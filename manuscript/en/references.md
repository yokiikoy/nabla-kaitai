# References (with Author's Comments)

The peculiar algebraic shortcut constructed in this book was built on the shoulders of giants. Understanding the differences in each author's approach is extremely valuable for surveying the ecosystem of physical mathematics.

---

**1. Daniel Fleisch, *A Student's Guide to Vectors and Tensors*, Cambridge University Press**

Comment: The quagmire of the old regime and the traditional approach. A traditional textbook on vector and tensor analysis that physics students first encounter. It tackles Christoffel symbols and complex component calculations head-on. This book 'Unmasking Div Grad Curl' consciously regards it as a typical example (anti-pattern) of the "memorization hell" from which it tries to save readers, though it is also an indispensable classic for mastering classical calculation techniques.

---

**2. David Bachman, *A Geometric Approach to Differential Forms*, Birkhäuser**

Comment: A visual and geometric approach to differential forms. An ambitious work that attempts to grasp differential forms visually through geometric images like "projections onto planes," rather than through axioms or algebra. I drew great inspiration from this book when forming the intuition in Chapters 1 and 2 that "$dx$ is a measuring device," but to reduce friction in component calculations, this book adopts that geometric intuition by translating it into "matrix calculations."

---

**3. Harley Flanders, *Differential Forms with Applications to the Physical Sciences*, Dover Publications**

Comment: A classical bible connecting pure mathematics and physics. A classic work for applying differential forms to physical sciences, containing all the rigorous operational rules of the exterior derivative $d$ and Hodge star $\ast$. However, it starts from the purely mathematical axiomatic approach such as dual spaces, making it a high barrier for beginners. This book aimed to bring readers up to Flanders's practical level in one fell swoop.

---

**4. William L. Burke, *Applied Differential Geometry*, Cambridge University Press; *Div, Grad, Curl are Dead* (draft)**

Comment: The spiritual backbone of this book — the philosophy of "delaying the metric." The most important reference that determined the skeleton of this book's structure. Burke's approach runs through the unfinished draft *Div, Grad, Curl are Dead* as well. It fiercely criticizes "metric-blinded symbol pushing" and advocates the powerful pedagogical philosophy of "putting the metric later and later into the course." The structure of this book — deferring the formal definition of the metric until Chapter 5, then introducing it discovery-style as $g = J^T J$ — is an homage to Burke's philosophy.

---

**5. Leonard Susskind & Art Friedman, *Quantum Mechanics: The Theoretical Minimum*, Basic Books**
**H. S. Green, *Matrix Mechanics*, P. Noordhoff LTD**

Comment: Hacking notation — the source of the first breakthrough. Texts for learning Dirac's "bra-ket notation" and matrix representation in quantum mechanics. The core idea of this book — translating the abstract 1-form (dual vector) into the physicist's "mother tongue": "lay a column vector on its side to become a row vector and take the matrix product" — was born by reverse-importing (hacking) these intuitions from quantum mechanics into the classical field.

---

**6. Chris Doran & Anthony Lasenby, *Geometric Algebra for Physicists*, Cambridge University Press**

Comment: The ultimate world beyond this book — the integration of metric and geometry. The next destination for readers who have finished this book: Geometric Algebra, at the frontiers of modern physics. While this book, following Burke's philosophy, thoroughly separated "metric" and "geometry" ($d$ and $\ast$), in Geometric Algebra they are completely integrated from the start as a single "geometric product." It is precisely because readers know the pain of separation that they can understand the true beauty of this integrated paradigm.
