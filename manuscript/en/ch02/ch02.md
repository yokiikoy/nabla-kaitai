# Chapter 2: Area and volume as algebra

In Chapter 1, we defined $dx, dy, dz$ as a row vector ($1$-form) that "eats" one vector and returns a scalar, and we reinterpreted $\int f\,dx$ as the limit of a matrix action. In this chapter, we extend this idea and **algebraically assemble an area meter ($2$-form) that eats two vectors and a volume meter ($3$-form) that eats three vectors**, using the same building blocks $dx, dy, dz$.

### §2.1 The limits of elementary school area

Many readers know that "integration is a calculation for finding area."
But what exactly is area? Upon reflection, you might realize that since elementary school, you've never really formalized it.

So what was that elementary school formalization? It was based on a very simple intuition: "how many $1 \times 1$ squares (tiles) can be packed into the shape?"

For figures drawn on a 2D sheet of paper (the $xy$ plane), this "tiling with squares" works well. For example, the area $S$ of the parallelogram spanned by two 2D vectors $\mathbf{v}_1 = (x_1, y_1)$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (x_2, y_2)$ can be written as follows:

$$S = \sqrt{(x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1)^2}$$

However, we live in 3D space. When we try to measure the area of a "tilted plane" in 3D space, things get a bit tricky.

Many readers may remember having to find the area of a parallelogram spanned by vectors $\mathbf{v}_1 = (x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (x_2, y_2, z_2)$ in three-dimensional space in high school math. Using high school notation, we can write:

$$
S = \sqrt{|\mathbf{v}_1|^2 |\mathbf{v}_2|^2 - (\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2)^2}
$$

Expanding into components involves a calculation that fills up a notebook page, until we finally arrive at the following formula.

$$S = \sqrt{(y_1z_2 - z_1y_2)^2 + (z_1x_2 - x_1z_2)^2 + (x_1y_2 - x_2y_1)^2}$$

The process is quite messy, with square roots and squares scattered everywhere.
Why does it become so complicated? Because we originally defined area **within the 2D world**. **In 3D space**, the expression balloons because it involves both the "tilt" of the plane and the relationship between the two vectors lying on it, including **handling angles**.

> <strong> Note </strong> (Anticipating undefined terms) In this book, we have not yet introduced <strong> directions, angles, or inner products </strong>. Yet here I freely use these words, but that should not hinder your reading of this discussion.

At this point, let's change our perspective completely. **Let's temporarily set aside the elementary school definition of area**, and **redesign from scratch an "algebraic measuring device that outputs area"** .

---

### §2.2 The three rules an area meter must satisfy

As the first step in the design, instead of relying only on "$1\times 1$ squares," we will take as our starting point the **"parallelogram spanned by two vectors"** .

> <strong> Note </strong> (Why start with a parallelogram) For readers who are used to "area = tiling with squares," the motivation for suddenly starting with a parallelogram might not be clear. Taking a parallelogram as the base allows us to algebraically handle the "intertwining" of two vectors, which cleans up the rules we are about to build. The square naturally appears as a **special case** (when the sides are orthogonal). Also, when **extending integrals to higher dimensions**, this approach is easier to handle.

To rephrase, what we want is a magic black box $S(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2)$ that takes two vectors $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$ as inputs and outputs the area $S$ of the parallelogram they span.

For this black box to function properly as an "area meter," it must satisfy at least the following three rules. Try to visualize a parallelogram whose adjacent sides are the two arrows emanating from the origin.

#### 2.2.1 Rule 1: If one side stretches, the area also stretches (proportionality)
If the length of vector $\mathbf{v}_1$ doubles, the area of the parallelogram should naturally double.
$$S(2\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) = 2 S(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2)$$
Similarly, if we input the sum of two vectors, the area should decompose into a sum:
$$S(\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}_2) = S(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) + S(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}_2)$$
These two properties together are called <strong> linearity </strong>. The same properties (scalar multiplication and distribution over addition) hold for the second argument $\mathbf{v}_2$ as well — that is, $S$ is <strong> linear in both arguments </strong> ( i.e., <strong> bilinear </strong> ).

#### 2.2.2 Rule 2: If they overlap, it's flat and zero (alternating property)
This is the most important rule. What if $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$ are exactly the same vector? The parallelogram collapses into a mere "line," and the area becomes zero.
$$S(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_1) = 0$$

In fact, from this rule of "same gives zero," a very strange property follows. Using Rule 1 (linearity), let's put $\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2$ into both arguments. Since "same gives zero," we have:

$$S(\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2,\; \mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2) = 0$$

Expanding the left side using linearity:

$$S(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_1) + S(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) + S(\mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_1) + S(\mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_2) = 0$$

The first and fourth terms vanish by Rule 2, so we are left with $S(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) + S(\mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_1) = 0$. That is:

$$S(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) = - S(\mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_1)$$

Swapping the order of the input vectors **reverses the sign of the area**.

> <strong> Note </strong> (Sign of area 1) You might be surprised: "Area becoming negative? That's a problem." But physicists do not ignore the "direction" in nature. The direction of electric current, the direction of magnetic fields — all depend essentially on the "direction" of vectors. So the idea that "area also has a direction (signed area)" is actually quite natural for us physicists. This concept will later become absolutely essential for distinguishing the "front" and "back" of surfaces, and for computing magnetic flux or fluid flow through a region. For now, please don't get too hung up on it and continue.

> <strong> Note </strong> (Sign of area 2) Even for a single variable, let's set $f(x)__PH_0104__基底ベクトル__PH_0105__（標準基底の一つ）$\hat{e}_x$ と $y$方向の基底ベクトル $\hat{e}_y$ が作る正方形の面積を $1$. Following the convention of this book, writing it as a <strong> column vector </strong> gives
$$\hat{e}_x = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \hat{e}_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
.

$$S(\hat{e}_x, \hat{e}_y) = 1$$

> <strong> Note </strong> (Basis vectors (standard basis)) The vectors of unit length along each axis of Cartesian coordinates are called basis vectors (standard basis). For a rigorous definition, we defer to linear algebra.

> <strong> [Checkpoint] </strong>
> - The area meter is a device that "eats two vectors and returns a scalar." It must satisfy three rules: linearity, alternation, and normalization.
> - From alternation (two identical vectors gives zero), we derive $S(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2) = -S(\mathbf{v}_2,\mathbf{v}_1)$, so area becomes **signed**.
> - In the next section, we will concretely search for an algebraic device that satisfies these three rules.

### §2.3 The true identity of the area (meter) is an "antisymmetric matrix"

Now, here comes the mathematical magic.
We set the three rules while picturing the **geometric image** of a "parallelogram." But once the rules are established, we no longer need to visualize the shape. **Because if we find an "algebraic formula that satisfies these three rules," that becomes the definition of area.**

To extract the essence, let's first consider vectors that always have zero $z$ component — that is, vectors lying in the $xy$ plane.

$$
\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ y_1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} x_2 \\ y_2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Let's jump to the conclusion. **Under this restriction of vectors in the $xy$ plane**, there exists exactly one device in the world of linear algebra that perfectly satisfies the three rules.

It is a calculation of the form "<strong> row vector </strong> times a specific <strong> $3 \times 3$ matrix </strong> times <strong> column vector </strong>," with that specific matrix inserted in the middle:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
  0 & 1 & 0 \\
  -1 & 0 & 0 \\
  0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} x_2 \\ y_2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Let's actually compute by substituting $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
  0 & 1 & 0 \\
  -1 & 0 & 0 \\
  0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} x_2 \\ y_2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} -y_1 & x_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} x_2 \\ y_2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
= x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1
$$

The output $x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1$ is exactly the same as the "2D area formula" we mentioned at the beginning!

<!-- concept-scope: allow(metric) -->
> <strong> Note </strong> (Apologies to mathematicians) We have just nonchalantly written the form "$\mathbf{v}_1^T M \mathbf{v}_2$ (row $\times$ matrix $\times$ column)." In strict pure mathematical style, this is bad behavior. Because underneath the action of laying a vector horizontally, an "inner product (metric)" that we have not yet defined in this book could sneak in. **But we are physicists.** Since it doesn't hinder the calculation, let's proceed pragmatically.

#### 2.3.1 Why anti-symmetric?

Let's return to the main thread. Look carefully at the structure of the matrix sandwiched in the middle.
The diagonal entries (from top-left to bottom-right) are all $0$, and on either side of the diagonal, $1$ and $-1$ face each other **with their signs reversed**. Such a matrix is called an "antisymmetric matrix" (alternating matrix).

This "antisymmetry" is the core of area.
As you can see from the formula $x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1$, this operation **subtracts** "$\mathbf{v}_1$'s $x$ times $\mathbf{v}_2$'s $y$" from "$\mathbf{v}_2$'s $x$ times $\mathbf{v}_1$'s $y$".
If $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$ are exactly the same vector, then $x_1 y_1 - x_1 y_1 = 0$, and they cancel out beautifully. The property of "sign reversal in the off-diagonal entries" of the matrix automatically handles the "zero if flat (cancellation)" of Rule 2 algebraically.

Why is it uniquely determined? By Rule 1 (linearity), the area of any pair of vectors can be decomposed into a sum of areas of basis vectors (standard basis). And Rule 3 (normalization) fixes the top-right entry to 1 as soon as we set the area of the unit square to "1". Furthermore, Rule 2 (alternation) forces the diagonal entries to be zero and forces the bottom-left entry to be -1. Notice that the third row and third column of the matrix are all zeros — because the $z$ component of the input vectors is always zero; any value placed there would not affect the result of the $\mathbf{v}_1^T M \mathbf{v}_2$ computation, so it is undetermined by the three rules alone. We can view the current discussion as embedding the $xy$ plane into $\mathbb{R}^3$, and thus the **redundant $z$ direction does not enter the measurement** — in that sense, setting them to zero is natural. In other words, **our physically motivated rules completely "designed" the shape of this matrix.**

#### 2.3.2 Evolution of the meter and the next wall

Here, let's make our position clear.
Rather than placing the naive intuition of counting **tiles** from elementary school first, **we define the quantity obtained by feeding two vectors into this antisymmetric matrix $3 \times 3$** as the **area component returned by this measuring device (an area meter dedicated to the $xy$ plane)** — a reading along $dx \wedge dy$. It is not that all **information** of signed area in 3D space is captured by a single number; rather, it is merely **a scalar extracted through one particular lens**. We will present the full picture in §2.4.5 when we have all three types of meters.

The <strong> $1$-form (1-form) </strong> of the previous chapter was a **"$1 \times 3$ matrix" that catches one vector.
The matrix we have before us now has **evolved into an "$3 \times 3$ antisymmetric matrix" that receives two vectors simultaneously and computes area.**

We will call this matrix (or operator) a **$2$-form**.

> <strong> Note </strong> (Quadratic form and $2$-form) In linear algebra, a "quadratic form" usually refers to a polynomial of degree two. The term <strong> $2$-form </strong> here comes from differential forms and is a different object.

Now, you are probably thinking:
"Okay, I understand that a matrix can give area. But this matrix has its third row and third column zero, completely ignoring the $z$ component. That doesn't solve the 'hard calculation' in 3D at all!"

**You are absolutely right.**
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ we have just obtained is only one of three basic measuring instruments in 3D space — **an area meter dedicated to the $xy$ plane**.
In other words, it is like **a "biased lens" that sees only the $xy$ plane**. In space, there also exist lenses for viewing the $yz$ plane and the $zx$ plane.

> - In 3D space, there are three types of "biased lenses" (basis $2$-forms).
> - In §2.4, we will reverse-engineer the area meter from rulers (tensor product and wedge product).

### §2.4 Internal structure of the area meter

#### 2.4.1 Determining the shape of a "device that handles two inputs"

In §2.3, we stated that the true identity of the area meter ($2$-form) is an "antisymmetric $3 \times 3$ matrix" sandwiched in the middle. However, that matrix was given to us as a gift from the author.

Our next ambition is this:
**"Can we systematically build that complicated matrix by combining the simple rulers $dx, dy, dz$ introduced in Chapter 1 as components?"**
If we can, then even though we currently only have an area meter for the $xy$ plane, we will be able to mass-produce area meters for the $yz$ plane and the $zx$ plane using the same components and the same procedure.

To achieve this, let's first consider what the outer frame of a "device that eats two vectors and spits out a scalar" should look like.

In Chapter 1, we saw that a device that eats one vector (a $1$-form) can be represented by a row vector (row $\times$ column $=$ scalar).
So, what about a device that simultaneously eats two vectors $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$, and preserves Rule 1 (proportionality/linearity) for both?

It must take the form of a rectangular table (matrix) sandwiched between the two vectors, i.e., **"row $\times$ matrix $\times$ column ($\mathbf{v}_1^T M \mathbf{v}_2$)"**. This is a requirement of linear algebra. What we need to find is the content of that matrix $M$ in the middle.

#### 2.4.2 Filling in the grid from the desired result

There's no need to search in the dark. We already know the **"answer"** that should be output:
the area formula for the $xy$ plane.

$$\text{欲しい出力} = x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1$$

To produce this output, what numbers should we place in the grid of the matrix $M$? Join me in solving this puzzle by imagining the expansion of $\mathbf{v}_1^T M \mathbf{v}_2$.

$$
\begin{pmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & z_1 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
  ? & ? & ? \\
  ? & ? & ? \\
  ? & ? & ?
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} x_2 \\ y_2 \\ z_2 \end{pmatrix}
$$

1. <strong> Creating $x_1 y_2$: </strong> At the place where the $x$ component of $\mathbf{v}_1$ ($x_1$) multiplies the $y$ component of $\mathbf{v}_2$ ($y_2$) — that is, at row 1, column 2 — we should put $+1$.
2. <strong> Creating $-x_2 y_1$: </strong> At the place where the $y$ component of $\mathbf{v}_1$ ($y_1$) multiplies the $x$ component of $\mathbf{v}_2$ ($x_2$) — that is, at row 2, column 1 — we should put $-1$.
3. <strong> Everywhere else: </strong> The area formula does not involve $x_1 x_2$ or $z$ components at all. Therefore, all remaining entries are $0$.

This is exactly how we obtained that antisymmetric matrix in §2.3, which was given as a gift!

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix}
  0 & 1 & 0 \\
  -1 & 0 & 0 \\
  0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
$$

#### 2.4.3 Introduction of the tensor product
Now, if we look closely at this area meter matrix $M$, we see that it is built from the "subtraction" of two parts.

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} - 
 \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

The first term on the right is a "matrix that has $1$ only at row 1, column 2". This represents a **one-way operation** that extracts the $x$ component from $\mathbf{v}_1$ and the $y$ component from $\mathbf{v}_2$ and multiplies them ($x_1 y_2$).

Recall the rulers from Chapter 1. The ruler that extracts the $x$ component was $dx$, and the ruler that extracts the $y$ component was $dy$.
Let's create a new operator by combining these two rulers: "measure $\mathbf{v}_1$ with $dx$, measure $\mathbf{v}_2$ with $dy$, and multiply". We call this the **tensor product**, and denote it by $dx \otimes dy$.

$$(dx \otimes dy)(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) := dx(\mathbf{v}_1)\,dy(\mathbf{v}_2) = x_1 y_2$$

The tensor product $dx \otimes dy$ sounds intimidating, but don't be afraid. In the world of matrix representation, it is simply a switch-like operation that **"makes the entry at row 1, column 2 equal to $1$"**.

> <strong> Note </strong> (Matrix representation of the tensor product) For readers who like formulas, when we set $dx = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $dy = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the matrix representation of $dx \otimes dy$ is obtained mechanically by "transposing the first vector (making it a column), placing it next to the second row vector ($dx^T dy$), and multiply every pair of components exhaustively to fill the matrix entries."

#### 2.4.4 Completion of the wedge product

Now we are ready.
The area meter matrix for the $xy$ plane we derived was the "switch for $x_1 y_2$" minus the "switch for $y_1 x_2$".

Translating this into the language of rulers ($1$-forms), the definition of a new operator, the **wedge product ($dx \wedge dy$)**, naturally emerges.

$$
dx \wedge dy := dx \otimes dy - dy \otimes dx
$$

The symbol $\wedge$ is pronounced "wedge". When we apply this definition to vectors, we get:

$$
(dx \wedge dy)(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) = dx(\mathbf{v}_1)\,dy(\mathbf{v}_2) - dy(\mathbf{v}_1)\,dx(\mathbf{v}_2) = x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1
$$

Beautifully, we have systematically constructed the area meter ($2$-form) from the combination of rulers ($1$-forms) of Chapter 1.
Note also that because it is constructed as a subtraction of tensor products, **Rule 2 of §2.2 — swapping arguments reverses the sign (alternating property) — is automatically "built in."**

#### 2.4.5 Three basis $2$-forms

We can construct the remaining two "biased lenses" in the same way.

**Lens for viewing the $yz$ plane:** $dy \wedge dz$

$$
dy \otimes dz - dz \otimes dy = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

**Lens for viewing the $zx$ plane:** $dz \wedge dx$

$$
dz \otimes dx - dx \otimes dz = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

**That's all of them!**

Now, let's apply each of the three lenses to general vectors and see what each measures.

For general 3D vectors $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ y_1 \\ z_1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} x_2 \\ y_2 \\ z_2 \end{pmatrix}$, we compute according to the definition in §2.4.4 (put $\mathbf{v}_1$ in the first argument and $\mathbf{v}_2$ in the second):

$$
(dy \wedge dz)(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) = y_1 z_2 - z_1 y_2
$$

$$
(dz \wedge dx)(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) = z_1 x_2 - x_1 z_2
$$

$$
(dx \wedge dy)(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) = x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1
$$

Each calculation is just one subtraction. No matter what the direction of the vectors is, the definition of the wedge product automatically returns the correct value.

#### 2.4.6 The true identity of signed area and the "basis on the figure side"

Now, you are probably expecting this: "If we combine the results of the three lenses somehow, we'll finally get a single scalar, right?"

Sorry, **I've actually been hiding something.**

When we designed the area meter in §2.2, for the case restricted to the $xy$ plane, indeed **one number** was enough. But for a general parallelogram in 3D space, orientation is not just a matter of two choices (like $+/-$). The three measured values through the three basis $2$-forms (lenses)
$$A_{yz} = (dy \wedge dz)(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2),\quad A_{zx} = (dz \wedge dx)(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2),\quad A_{xy} = (dx \wedge dy)(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2)$$
, taken together, constitute the necessary information for "signed area."

At this point, a sharp reader should arrive at a question:
"These three numbers — **coefficients with respect to what basis** are they?"

Recall from Chapter 1: we learned that the _x_ component of the measuring device (row vector) $dx$, a $1$-form, and the figure side (column vector) $\hat{e}_x$ (a standard basis vector) are paired. By combining the row and column, $dx$ eats $\hat{e}_x$ and returns the scalar $1$ ($dx(\hat{e}_x) = 1$).

So, for the area meter ($2$-form) $dy \wedge dz$, which eats $1$ and returns $\alpha\,dy \wedge dz + \cdots$, what is the **"basis on the figure (column) side"** ? If we built the device by connecting row vectors with the wedge product $\wedge$, **then we should also connect column vectors with $\wedge$!**

That is, the basis on the figure side is something called a "bivector" or "area meter", written as $\hat{e}_y \wedge \hat{e}_z$.
Using this, the true identity of the measured "signed area" can be written as a concrete entity (figure) as follows:

$$\text{向き付き面積} = A_{yz}\,(\hat{e}_y \wedge \hat{e}_z) + A_{zx}\,(\hat{e}_z \wedge \hat{e}_x) + A_{xy}\,(\hat{e}_x \wedge \hat{e}_y)$$

Now everything should be perfectly clear.
A general __PH_0548__ with coefficient fields is **the "area meter (scale)"** , while $A_{yz}(\hat{e}_y \wedge \hat{e}_z) + \cdots$ with the numerical measurement results placed inside is **the "measured figure (area itself)"** . They are in a row-versus-column relationship and must never be confused.

> <strong> Note </strong> (Where did the matrix go?) In the previous sections we kept saying "the area meter is an antisymmetric matrix." You may wonder what happens to the matrix representation on the figure side. In 3D space, the bivector on the figure side is also isomorphic to a $3 \times 3$ antisymmetric matrix (a matrix with components $A_{yz}$ etc.). However, explicitly showing this matrix representation here might give the misleading impression that one could add or multiply it with the "area meter's matrix," which would harm intuition. So in this book, we will stop with the explanation that "a figure rides on a basis of bivectors."

#### 2.4.7 Returning to the elementary school scalar area

Now, let's return to the "positive area" we learned in elementary school.

You probably know that to extract the "length (scalar)" from a 1D figure data — a vector $\mathbf{v} = x\hat{e}_x + y\hat{e}_y + z\hat{e}_z$ — we compute the square root of the sum of squares of the coefficients on each basis: $\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$.

The operation to extract the "magnitude (scalar area)" of a bivector, which is a 2D figure data, takes the same form. For the three coefficients $A_{yz}, A_{zx}, A_{xy}$ of the bases $\hat{e}_y \wedge \hat{e}_z,\,\hat{e}_z \wedge \hat{e}_x,\,\hat{e}_x \wedge \hat{e}_y$ that constitute the signed area, we simply take the square root of the sum of squares directly, without rearranging them into a column vector.

$$S = \sqrt{A_{yz}^2 + A_{zx}^2 + A_{xy}^2} = \sqrt{(y_1 z_2 - z_1 y_2)^2 + (z_1 x_2 - x_1 z_2)^2 + (x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1)^2}$$

This exactly matches the "tough calculation" area formula from high school that we called "the hard way" in §2.1. Starting from the design of an algebraic measuring device, we have safely returned to the elementary school "tile count."

#### 2.4.8 1D "area" (length) and 0D "area" (point)

Let's take a step down in dimension. You said the operation "square root of sum of squares" looked familiar, but in fact we already know the same "scale" and "figure" structure for **1D figures (line segments)**.

The $1$-form ($dx, dy, dz$) we dealt with in Chapter 1 was a measuring device that "eats just one vector."
The figure being measured is the vector itself ($\mathbf{v} = x\hat{e}_x + y\hat{e}_y + z\hat{e}_z$), which is precisely the data of "1D signed area (signed length)." The operation to extract the "scalar length" from this was $\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$.

Then what is a **$0$-form**?
Since "a device that eats $k$ vectors is a $k$-form," a $0$-form would be **a device that eats zero vectors**.
It requires no input direction (arrow); just by being placed there, it returns a single scalar (temperature, density, etc.) — that is simply a **"scalar field"** .
The "0D figure" that a $0$-form measures is just a "point"; it has only one component, and its magnitude is simply its absolute value.

Point (0D), line segment (1D), parallelogram (2D). They are all repetitions of exactly the same structure: the relationship between the "scale ($k$-form)" and the "figure ($k$-vector)" as row and column vectors.

In §2.5, we will raise this one more level and apply the same structure to the 3D parallelepiped (volume). To summarize:
- **$k=0$**: A $0$-form measures a point. 1 component, magnitude = absolute value.
- **$k=1$**: A $1$-form measures a vector (**signed length**). 3 components, magnitude = $\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$.
- **$k=2$**: A $2$-form measures a bivector (**signed area**). 3 components, magnitude = $\sqrt{A_{yz}^2+A_{zx}^2+A_{xy}^2}$.
- **$k=3$**: A $3$-form measures a trivector (**signed volume**). 1 component, magnitude = absolute value.

Since the stage of this book is 3D space, we stop at $k=0,1,2,3$, but the principle that "a $k$-form eats a $k$-vector and returns a scalar" does not depend on $k$. This pattern will be carried through to integration in Chapter 3 and to exterior derivatives in Chapter 4.

#### 2.4.9 Correspondence with the cross product

Readers who have learned the "vector cross product (cross product)" in university linear algebra might recognize the following three components:

$$\mathbf{v}_1 \times \mathbf{v}_2 = (y_1 z_2 - z_1 y_2)\,\hat{e}_x + (z_1 x_2 - x_1 z_2)\,\hat{e}_y + (x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1)\,\hat{e}_z = \begin{pmatrix} y_1 z_2 - z_1 y_2 \\ z_1 x_2 - x_1 z_2 \\ x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1 \end{pmatrix}$$

These are none other than the **values** of $dy \wedge dz$, $dz \wedge dx$, $dx \wedge dy$ arranged in this order.

> <strong> Note </strong> (For anxious readers) Even if you don't recognize it, it doesn't hinder your understanding from here on; we just discovered the wedge product first.

> <strong> Note </strong> (Quadratic forms and cross product) Between the area as a quadratic form and the cross product as a 3-component vector, there is a relationship that will be named **Hodge duality** in a later chapter. We will clarify the detailed correspondence and why it is both necessary and wonderful later; for now, just think of it as "a difference in how the information of signed area is stored."

---

> <strong> [Checkpoint] </strong>
> - The wedge product $dx \wedge dy := dx \otimes dy - dy \otimes dx$ automatically generates an antisymmetric matrix by subtracting tensor products. Similarly, $dy \wedge dz$ and $dz \wedge dx$ are constructed, and any area meter can be built as a linear combination of the three basis $2$-forms.
> - The true nature of signed area is a bivector: the measured values $A_{yz}, A_{zx}, A_{xy}$ multiplied by the figure-side bases $\hat{e}_y \wedge \hat{e}_z$, $\hat{e}_z \wedge \hat{e}_x$, $\hat{e}_x \wedge \hat{e}_y$ — the scale (basis $2$-forms) and the figure ($k$-vectors) are in a row-column relationship, never to be confused.
> - The scalar area (the positive magnitude corresponding to tile count) is recovered as the square root of the sum of squares of the coefficients, $\sqrt{A_{yz}^2 + A_{zx}^2 + A_{xy}^2}$, which matches the formula from §2.1 (Lagrange's identity). It is the same kind of operation as the formula for vector length.

### §2.5 Volume meter and the determinant

In the previous section, we multiplied two 1-forms (rulers) $dx, dy$ with the wedge product to assemble an area meter ($2$-form) $dx \wedge dy$. The area meter was "a device that eats two vectors and returns the signed area of the parallelogram they span."

Now it's time for **volume**. The stance here is the same. First, we assign volume $1$ to the **unit cube formed by the standard basis $\hat{e}_x,\hat{e}_y,\hat{e}_z$** (Rule 3), and then determine the measuring device through alternation and linearity. To return to the **positive volume** of the elementary school "how many unit cubes fit," we again take the **square root of the sum of squares of the components** (or absolute value) — the same structure as for area.

A natural question arises.
**"Can a device that measures the volume of a parallelepiped spanned by three vectors — a volume meter ($3$-form) — be assembled in the same way?"**

#### 2.5.1 Rules the volume meter must satisfy

We will take exactly the same strategy as when we designed the area meter in §2.2. First, decide "the rules that the volume meter $V$ must satisfy," and then uniquely derive the form of the device from the rules.

The volume meter $V(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3)$ is a function that eats three vectors and returns a single scalar. The rules we impose on it are a **direct extension of the three rules for the area meter to three arguments**:

**Rule 1 (Linear in each argument)**
If one vector doubles, the volume doubles. Also distributes over addition of vectors.
$$V(a\mathbf{v}_1 + b\mathbf{u},\; \mathbf{v}_2,\; \mathbf{v}_3) = a\,V(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3) + b\,V(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3)$$
Similarly for the second and third arguments.

**Rule 2 (Alternating — swapping any two reverses the sign)**
$$V(\mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_3) = -V(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3)$$
Similarly for any other pair. By the same reasoning as in §2.2, **putting the same vector into two slots always gives zero** — if $\mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{v}_2$, then $V(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_3) = -V(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_3)$, so $V=0$. Geometrically, the parallelepiped collapses, but **algebraically, alternation alone forces it to be zero**. This is the same structure as Rule 2 of the 2D area meter.

**Rule 3 (Normalization — volume of the unit cube is 1)**
$$V(\hat{e}_x, \hat{e}_y, \hat{e}_z) = 1$$

#### 2.5.2 Determining the form from the rules — basis expansion and elimination

We'll follow the same procedure as when we derived the area meter matrix in §2.3. Expand the three vectors in the basis:
$$\mathbf{v}_1 = x_1 \hat{e}_x + y_1 \hat{e}_y + z_1 \hat{e}_z, \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = x_2 \hat{e}_x + y_2 \hat{e}_y + z_2 \hat{e}_z, \quad \mathbf{v}_3 = x_3 \hat{e}_x + y_3 \hat{e}_y + z_3 \hat{e}_z$$

By Rule 1 (multilinearity), $V(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3)$ expands into $3 \times 3 \times 3 = 27$ terms:
$$V(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3) = \sum_{i,j,k} v_{1i}\,v_{2j}\,v_{3k}\; V(\hat{e}_i, \hat{e}_j, \hat{e}_k)$$
Here, $i, j, k$ are indices that each **independently** run through $x, y, z$ ("running" means each takes one of the three values as we sum).

27 terms is a lot, but Rule 2 (alternation) eliminates many terms:
- **All terms with any repeated index are zero** (e.g., $V(\hat{e}_x, \hat{e}_x, \hat{e}_z) = 0$)
- Combinations without repetition are only when $i, j, k$ are all different, i.e., a **permutation** of $(x,y,z)$.

There are $3! = 6$ permutations of $(x, y, z)$. Due to alternation, even permutations have the same sign as $V(\hat{e}_x, \hat{e}_y, \hat{e}_z)$, odd permutations the opposite sign. By Rule 3, $V(\hat{e}_x, \hat{e}_y, \hat{e}_z) = 1$:

| Permutation | Even/Odd | Value |
|------|-------|----|
| $(x,y,z)$ | Even (0 swaps) | $+1$ |
| $(y,z,x)$ | Even (2 swaps) | $+1$ |
| $(z,x,y)$ | Even (2 swaps) | $+1$ |
| $(y,x,z)$ | Odd (1 swap) | $-1$ |
| $(x,z,y)$ | Odd (1 swap) | $-1$ |
| $(z,y,x)$ | Odd (1 swap) | $-1$ |

Therefore:
$$V(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3) = x_1 y_2 z_3 + y_1 z_2 x_3 + z_1 x_2 y_3 - y_1 x_2 z_3 - x_1 z_2 y_3 - z_1 y_2 x_3$$

If you've studied linear algebra, this looks like the **determinant** of the matrix formed by putting the three vectors in columns:
$$\begin{pmatrix} x_1 & x_2 & x_3 \\ y_1 & y_2 & y_3 \\ z_1 & z_2 & z_3 \end{pmatrix}$$
If you haven't, just **agree to call this sum of six terms the "determinant"** and proceed — the essential point is that this form emerged from the rules above.

The key point here is just one: the determinant is not merely "a procedure of multiplying and subtracting components," but can also be read as an operator that returns **a single number — the signed volume — when fed three column vectors in this order**. The $1$-form from Chapter 1 was "one vector → scalar"; the $2$-form from §2.3 was "two vectors → scalar"; the determinant is "three vectors → scalar," continuing this sequence.

For the area meter, an antisymmetric matrix emerged; for the volume meter, the determinant emerged. **In both cases, the rules we imposed determined the algebraic form.**

> <strong> Note </strong> (Order of the six terms of the determinant) Some textbooks simply state "the definition of the determinant is these six terms." In this book's flow, it is **Rules for the measuring device → six terms**.

#### 2.5.3 Reinterpreting the $2$-form as a determinant

In fact, the wedge product of $2$-forms defined in §2.4 also had a determinant hidden inside. Recall the definition:
$$(dx \wedge dy)(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) = dx(\mathbf{v}_1)\,dy(\mathbf{v}_2) - dy(\mathbf{v}_1)\,dx(\mathbf{v}_2)$$

The right side is none other than the following $2 \times 2$ determinant:
$$(dx \wedge dy)(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) = \det\begin{pmatrix} dx(\mathbf{v}_1) & dx(\mathbf{v}_2) \\ dy(\mathbf{v}_1) & dy(\mathbf{v}_2) \end{pmatrix}$$

In other words, the value of the wedge product is the determinant of a matrix whose rows are "which ruler" and columns are "which vector."

#### 2.5.4 Definition of $dx \wedge dy \wedge dz$ — completing the determinant pattern

By extending this "determinant pattern" straightforwardly to $3 \times 3$, we obtain the volume meter.

**Definition:** For three vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3$,
$$(dx \wedge dy \wedge dz)(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3) := \det\begin{pmatrix} dx(\mathbf{v}_1) & dx(\mathbf{v}_2) & dx(\mathbf{v}_3) \\ dy(\mathbf{v}_1) & dy(\mathbf{v}_2) & dy(\mathbf{v}_3) \\ dz(\mathbf{v}_1) & dz(\mathbf{v}_2) & dz(\mathbf{v}_3) \end{pmatrix}$$

Each component on the right is "the coordinate extracted from vector $\mathbf{v}_i$ by the 1-form $dx, dy, dz$," so this is exactly:
$$= \det\begin{pmatrix} x_1 & x_2 & x_3 \\ y_1 & y_2 & y_3 \\ z_1 & z_2 & z_3 \end{pmatrix}$$

This **perfectly matches the $V$ derived in §2.5.2. The volume meter can be constructed as the wedge product of three rulers $dx, dy, dz$.**

If you've learned about determinants in linear algebra, you can easily see that this definition satisfies the three rules of §2.5.1:
- **Multilinearity**: The determinant is linear in each column, so it is automatically satisfied.
- **Alternation**: Swapping two columns of a determinant reverses its sign.
- **Normalization**: Putting in $\hat{e}_x, \hat{e}_y, \hat{e}_z$ gives the identity matrix, so $\det(I) = 1$.

#### 2.5.5 Tensor product representation of the volume meter — an array of $3 \times 3$ blocks laid horizontally

In §2.4.3–2.4.4, we saw that the area meter $dx \wedge dy$ can be constructed from the subtraction of tensor products $dx \otimes dy - dy \otimes dx$, and the result is an antisymmetric $3 \times 3$ matrix. Can the same procedure be applied to the volume meter $dx \wedge dy \wedge dz$?

Why bring up tensor arrays here? — **To show that the operation of eating three vectors and returning a determinant can be written from the same unified perspective of "index contraction" as the area meter.** Understanding this reveals that $1$-forms, $2$-forms, and $3$-forms all operate under the same principle.

The answer is yes. However, while the tensor product of two rulers settled into a $3 \times 3$ matrix, for three rulers we get a $3 \times 3 \times 3 = 27$ component **3D array** because the number of indices increases by one. Such multi-dimensional arrays with three indices are called **tensors** in both mathematics and physics. A matrix (with two indices) is a special case of a tensor; what appears here is a **tensor of rank 3** (third-order tensor).

Incidentally, for tensors of rank 3 or higher, writing out all components would cause the page to explode, so even physicists rarely display them in array form. Instead, it is usual to write with a representative with indices, like component $\epsilon_{ijk}$, and leave the subsequent calculations to index summation rules. We'll follow that convention in this section and proceed by pulling out respective components as needed.

> <strong> Note </strong> (Tensor notation) In tensor algebra, the **notation itself** $\epsilon_{ijk}$ often denotes "the whole third-order tensor." To avoid confusion, this book will write **the entire third-order tensor** as $\widehat{\epsilon}$ and **a single component** as $\epsilon_{ijk}$. This means we do not identify the matrix representation (like $3\times3$ in §2.3) with a "single component." Be careful when reading other books.

> <strong> Note </strong> (Upper and lower indices) As an apology to mathematicians peeking at this book: in rigorous differential geometry and tensor analysis, it is common to distinguish vectors and covectors, bases and dual bases by using **upper and lower index positions**. Since this book fixes **Cartesian orthogonal coordinates** and works under the standard metric, we **deliberately do not show** the distinction between covector components and vector components — we use **all lower indices** like $\epsilon_{ijk}$.

Now, when we antisymmetrize this 27-component array (summing the six permutations with signs), we obtain an array where only 6 positions out of 27 become $\pm 1$ and the rest are zero. This is exactly the table of permutations derived in §2.5.2 — the components are as follows (the $(i,j,k)$ component of $\widehat{\epsilon}$):

$$
\epsilon_{ijk} = \begin{cases} +1 & (i,j,k) \text{ が } (x,y,z) \text{ の偶置換} \\ -1 & (i,j,k) \text{ が } (x,y,z) \text{ の奇置換} \\ 0 & \text{添字に重複あり} \end{cases}
$$

How should we display the arrangement of these 27 numbers? A good way is to think of it as **three $3 \times 3$ matrices laid out side by side**.

$$
\widehat{\epsilon} \;\longleftrightarrow\;
\begin{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} &
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} &
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

The first index $i$ specifies "which slice from the left," and each slice is a $3 \times 3$ block indexed by $j,k$.

Now, if we set
$$\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ y_1 \\ z_1 \end{pmatrix}$$
then, extending the analogy of a $1$-form (row vector) eating a column vector and returning a scalar, we contract by multiplying and summing the $M_i$-th block $i$ from the left with the component $v_{1i}$ using index $i$ — that is,
$$
M := x_1 M_1 + y_1 M_2 + z_1 M_3 = \sum_{i=1}^3 v_{1i}\, M_i
$$
and the result drops down to a single $3 \times 3$ matrix.
Incidentally, these three blocks turn out to be **the very area meter matrices** constructed in §2.4.4–2.4.5, with the following correspondence:

| Slice $i$ from left | Identity |
|:---:|:---:|
| $i=1$ | $dy \wedge dz$ (area meter for the $yz$ plane) |
| $i=2$ | $dz \wedge dx$ (area meter for the $zx$ plane) |
| $i=3$ | $dx \wedge dy$ (area meter for the $xy$ plane) |

**Crushing three vectors into a scalar**

The procedure of substituting $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3$ can be seen quickly as **multiplying the array's indices with the vector components where they match, and summing**:

$$
V(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3) = \sum_{i,j,k} \epsilon_{ijk}\, v_{1i}\, v_{2j}\, v_{3k}
$$

- **Step 1 (3D array → matrix):** Contract over the index $i$ by weighting and summing the three area meter matrices with the components of $\mathbf{v}_1$ — i.e., $\displaystyle M = \sum_{i=1}^3 v_{1i}\,\epsilon_{i,\cdot,\cdot}$ (where $\epsilon_{i,\cdot,\cdot}$ is the $3 \times 3$ block from the $i$-th slice of $\widehat{\epsilon}$). The result descends to a single $3 \times 3$ matrix $M$.
- **Step 2 (matrix → scalar):** Do "row $\times$ matrix $\times$ column" with the remaining two vectors — i.e., $V = \mathbf{v}_2^T\, M\, \mathbf{v}_3$. This is the operation we got used to in §2.4.

> <strong> Note </strong> (Contraction) The operation of summing over a common index to reduce dimensionality is called **contraction**. In fact, inner products and matrix multiplication can also be seen as types of contraction.

A detailed verification that this procedure reproduces every single one of the six terms of the determinant from §2.5.2, along with a numerical check, is provided in **the appendix to Chapter 2** by writing out all components.

#### 2.5.6 Concrete example: signed volume of a parallelepiped

Let's verify by doing a concrete calculation. Add a third vector to the two vectors whose area we measured in §2.4.5:

$$\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}$$

Feed them into the volume meter:
$$(dx \wedge dy \wedge dz)(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3) = \det\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$$

Computing this according to the definition:
$$= 1\cdot1\cdot0 + 0\cdot1\cdot1 + 1\cdot0\cdot1 - 1\cdot1\cdot1 - 0\cdot0\cdot0 - 1\cdot1\cdot1 = 0 + 0 + 0 - 1 - 0 - 1 = -2$$

> <strong> Note </strong> (Negative volume?) Just like the signed area in §2.2, the **order** can lead to a minus sign.

Try swapping $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$:
$$(dx \wedge dy \wedge dz)(\mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_3) = -(-2) = +2$$

#### 2.5.7 Correspondence with the scalar triple product

Readers who have studied the "scalar triple product" in vector calculus may know the following identity:
$$\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot (\mathbf{v}_2 \times \mathbf{v}_3) = \det\begin{pmatrix}x_1 & x_2 & x_3 \\ y_1 & y_2 & y_3 \\ z_1 & z_2 & z_3\end{pmatrix}$$

The right side is exactly our defined $(dx \wedge dy \wedge dz)(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3)$.

> <strong> Note </strong> (Anticipating the dot product) The " $\cdot$ " on the left is the symbol for the **inner product**, which we have not yet defined in this book. This is just a preview for readers who know it.

#### 2.5.8 The "basis on the figure side" for volume

As in the case of area (2D), we should clearly separate the "scale" and the "figure" here.

The $dx \wedge dy \wedge dz$ we have constructed is a **volume meter ($3$-form)** that waits for three vectors.
In contrast, the "parallelepiped itself as a figure (signed volume)" formed by three vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3$ rides on a basis of **"trivector (volume meter)"** created by the wedge product of column vectors.

In 3D space, there is only one type of basis for the volume meter: $\hat{e}_x \wedge \hat{e}_y \wedge \hat{e}_z$ (changing the order only flips the sign).
Therefore, the measured "signed volume" data can be written as follows:

$$\text{向き付き体積} = V\,(\hat{e}_x \wedge \hat{e}_y \wedge \hat{e}_z)$$

The coefficient $V$ is the value of the determinant computed by plugging the vectors into the volume meter (e.g., $-2$ in the example of §2.5.6).
And if you want the "elementary school positive volume (scalar volume)", you take the square root of the sum of squares of the components ($\sqrt{V^2} = |V|$). Since there is only one independent component, it becomes just the absolute value.

#### 2.5.9 The "size" of $3$-forms — why only one independent component

The area meter ($2$-form) could be represented by an antisymmetric $3 \times 3$ matrix, and it had three independent components (coefficients of $dy \wedge dz$, $dz \wedge dx$, $dx \wedge dy$).

How many independent components does the volume meter ($3$-form) have? Consider possible candidates for basis $3$-forms. How many ways are there to choose **three distinct** rulers from $dx, dy, dz$? … Only one: $dx \wedge dy \wedge dz$ (changing the order only flips the sign, but doesn't create a new basis).

> <strong> Note </strong> (Foreshadowing Hodge duality) You may have noticed: unlike area, the orientation of volume collapsed into a single scalar $+/-$. We didn't have to repeat the "confession" we made for area in §2.4.6, because the independent components of $3$-form happens to be exactly 1. And this is no coincidence. A $0$-form (scalar field) also has 1 independent component, a $1$-form has 3, a $2$-form also has 3 — that is, the counts are mirrored from the ends: $1, 3, 3, 1$. This arrangement is a foretaste of a symmetry called **Hodge duality**. Details in a later chapter.

#### 2.5.10 Hierarchy of measuring instruments — summary of the tools gained in Chapter 2

Now, let's take an overview of the "measuring instruments" we have built up from Chapter 1.

From §2.4.8 onward, especially through §2.5.8 (trivector on the figure side) and §2.5.9 (number of independent components), we see that **for each dimension $k$, the row-column relationship between the "scale ($k$-form)" and the "figure ($k$-vector)" repeats in the same pattern**. The number of data components and the method of extracting the "scalar (positive magnitude)" are summarized in the following table:

| Dimension ($k$) | Scale ($k$-form) | Figure ($k$-vector) | Data output | Extraction of scalar magnitude |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **0D** | $0$-form (scalar field $f$) | point | 1 component ($f$) | $\sqrt{f^2}$ |
| **1D** | $1$-form ($dx, dy, dz$) | vector ($\hat{e}_x$, etc.) | 3 components ($x, y, z$) | $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$ |
| **2D** | $2$-form ($dy \wedge dz$, etc.) | bivector ($\hat{e}_y \wedge \hat{e}_z$, etc.) | 3 coefficients ($A_{yz}, A_{zx}, A_{xy}$) | $\sqrt{A_{yz}^2 + A_{zx}^2 + A_{xy}^2}$ |
| **3D** | $3$-form ($dx \wedge dy \wedge dz$) | trivector ($\hat{e}_x \wedge \hat{e}_y \wedge \hat{e}_z$) | 1 component ($V$) | $\sqrt{V^2}$ |

> <strong> [Checkpoint] </strong>
> - The volume meter $dx \wedge dy \wedge dz$ ($3$-form) is the wedge product of three 1-forms, and its value equals the $3 \times 3$ determinant.
> - There is a $1, 3, 3, 1$ symmetry: $0$-form (1 component) → $1$-form (3 components) → $2$-form (3 components) → $3$-form (1 component).
> - The row-column relationship between scale ($k$-form) and figure ($k$-vector) repeats in the same pattern across dimensions. The scalar magnitude is extracted by taking the square root of the sum of squares of the components.

## Appendix to Chapter 2: Tensor product representation of the volume meter — full component calculation

In §2.5.5, we stated that contracting $\widehat{\epsilon}$ (component $\epsilon_{ijk}$) with three vectors yields the determinant. Here, we manually trace the entire process and verify every term.

#### Extension of the tensor product to three arguments

Recall: the $(i,j)$ component of $dx \otimes dy$ was the product of the $i$-component of $dx$ and the $j$-component of $dy$:
$$(dx \otimes dy)_{ij} = (dx)_i\,(dy)_j$$

The extension to three rulers is natural — just add one more index:
$$(dx \otimes dy \otimes dz)_{ijk} = (dx)_i\,(dy)_j\,(dz)_k$$

Substituting $dx = (1\ 0\ 0)$, $dy = (0\ 1\ 0)$, $dz = (0\ 0\ 1)$, out of the $3^3 = 27$ entries, only one — $(i,j,k) = (1,2,3)$ — is $1$, and the remaining 26 are all $0$. The other five permutations (e.g., $dx \otimes dz \otimes dy$) each have exactly one non-zero entry as well: for $dy \otimes dx \otimes dz$, it is at position $(2,1,3)$; for $dz \otimes dy \otimes dx$, at $(3,2,1)$, etc., each with $1$.

#### Antisymmetrization — superposing the six arrays with signs

In §2.4.4 for area, we had $dx \wedge dy = dx \otimes dy - dy \otimes dx$ (signed sum of two terms). For three rulers, we consider all permutations of the three rulers — in the Cartesian coordinates of the Euclidean space at hand, the rulers are $dx, dy, dz$. There are $3! = 6$ permutations. For each permutation $\sigma$, we take the tensor product $\otimes$ of the rulers in the permuted order, attach the sign $\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)$, and sum over all permutations $S_3$ — that is, the following sum of six lines, summarized as a single line in permutation language.

> <strong> Note </strong> (Symmetric group and sign of a permutation) $S_3$ is the third symmetric group (the set of permutations). $\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)$ is defined as $+1$ for **even permutations** and $-1$ for **odd permutations**.

Writing it out:

$$\underbrace{dx \otimes dy \otimes dz}_{(1,2,3)\text{ に }+1} - \underbrace{dx \otimes dz \otimes dy}_{(1,3,2)\text{ に }-1} + \underbrace{dy \otimes dz \otimes dx}_{(2,3,1)\text{ に }+1}$$
$$- \underbrace{dy \otimes dx \otimes dz}_{(2,1,3)\text{ に }-1} + \underbrace{dz \otimes dx \otimes dy}_{(3,1,2)\text{ に }+1} - \underbrace{dz \otimes dy \otimes dx}_{(3,2,1)\text{ に }-1}$$

When we superpose the six "arrays that are non-zero in only one place each" with signs, out of the 27 components, only 6 become $\pm 1$ and the remaining 21 become zero. Compare this with the permutation table in §2.5.2 — this is the construction principle that gives each component $\epsilon_{ijk}$ of $\widehat{\epsilon}$.

#### Writing out the component matrices

Let's verify the three component matrices shown in §2.5.5 cell by cell here.

**First component ($i = 1$, $x$):** Among the six terms, those containing $i = 1$ are from $(1,2,3)$ with $+1$ and from $(1,3,2)$ with $-1$.

$$\epsilon_{1,\cdot,\cdot} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Second component ($i = 2$, $y$):** Those containing $(2,3,1)$ are from $+1$ and from $(2,1,3)$ with $-1$.

$$\epsilon_{2,\cdot,\cdot} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Third component ($i = 3$, $z$):** Those containing $(3,1,2)$ are from $+1$ and from $(3,2,1)$ with $-1$.

$$\epsilon_{3,\cdot,\cdot} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

To repeat, these are respectively the matrices of $dy \wedge dz$, $dz \wedge dx$, and $dx \wedge dy$ themselves.

#### Feeding three vectors — the full contraction process

**Step 1: Contract over index $i$ by weighting with the components of $\mathbf{v}_1$ (crushing index $i$)**

Summing the three area meter matrices weighted by the components of $\mathbf{v}_1$. The 3D array eats one vector and descends to a $3 \times 3$ matrix:

$$M := \sum_i v_{1i}\, \epsilon_{i,\cdot,\cdot} = x_1 \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + y_1 \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + z_1 \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

Summing the components:

$$M = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & z_1 & -y_1 \\ -z_1 & 0 & x_1 \\ y_1 & -x_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

A "custom area meter for vector $\mathbf{v}_1$" emerges, mixed from the three area meters with the components of $\mathbf{v}_1$. Note that it is antisymmetric — the antisymmetry with respect to the indices of $\widehat{\epsilon}$ is inherited.

**Step 2: Sandwich the matrix with the remaining two vectors (crushing index $j, k$)**

Now it's the familiar "row $\times$ matrix $\times$ column" from §2.4:

$$V = \mathbf{v}_2^T\, M\, \mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{pmatrix} x_2 & y_2 & z_2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & z_1 & -y_1 \\ -z_1 & 0 & x_1 \\ y_1 & -x_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_3 \\ y_3 \\ z_3 \end{pmatrix}$$

First compute $\mathbf{v}_2^T \times M$ (row $\times$ matrix → row):

$$= \begin{pmatrix} y_1 z_2 - z_1 y_2, & z_1 x_2 - x_1 z_2, & x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Finally, sum the products of the resulting row vector's components with the corresponding components of $\mathbf{v}_3$ (row $\times$ column → scalar):

$$V = (y_1 z_2 - z_1 y_2)\,x_3 + (z_1 x_2 - x_1 z_2)\,y_3 + (x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1)\,z_3$$

Expanding and rearranging:

$$= x_1 y_2 z_3 + y_1 z_2 x_3 + z_1 x_2 y_3 - y_1 x_2 z_3 - x_1 z_2 y_3 - z_1 y_2 x_3$$

which **exactly matches the six terms of the determinant from §2.5.2, every single one.**
