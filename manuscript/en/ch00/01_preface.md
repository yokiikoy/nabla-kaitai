# Preface: A Gift to My Former Self, or a Line of Defense in the Age of Social Media

This book was written as a book for answering the questions I myself had as an undergraduate. It assumes roughly the background knowledge that my former self had at the time.

At the same time, once one publishes something mathematical-looking on the internet, a few lines of defense become necessary. There are places in this book where a more advanced textbook would begin by abstracting everything away, while I deliberately begin with matrix representations and computations on finite cells.

This is not meant to reject the more advanced point of view. Quite the opposite. The aim is to cultivate concrete tools, intuition, and a nose for “rigor” that one can rely on without fear when eventually facing the world beyond this book.

The detailed problem setting will be given in the next section, “Introduction.”

> <strong>Note</strong> (on notes)
> This book contains many notes. Some readers may find them unkind, since they interrupt the flow of the main text.
>
> In this book, however, notes have three roles.
>
> First, they are lines of defense for myself. They leave a small record, beside the main text, of where something is being omitted, where two things are being identified, and what kind of rigorous formulation lies further ahead.
>
> Second, they are meant to train the reader’s nose. Even if you do not understand everything yet, it helps to sense that “something is going on here” or that “this way of speaking has its limits.” That sense will become useful when you move on.
>
> Third, they are meant to work a little like flashbacks in a mystery novel. Notes that at first look like fragments gradually acquire meaning as you read on, until later you realize, “Ah, so that was what this was for.” I am aiming for a little of that reading experience as well.
>
> The notes are not there so that you understand everything on the spot. Pick up the parts you can read, and feel free to pass over the parts you cannot.

> <strong>Note</strong> (historical order and understandable order)
> Modern readers can learn matrices, linear algebra, differential forms, and vector analysis as already-organized tools. At least in the education I received, matrices first appeared in high school as algebraic tools one could compute with by hand. In the actual history, however, these tools seem to have developed back and forth, influencing one another as they went. Because of this, I think some confusion born of historical circumstances still remains in education. This book is not a book that reconstructs the history of mathematical physics. Rather, it is an attempt to unravel multivariable calculus and vector analysis in a different order, using the language of matrix algebra that modern readers already have.

> <strong>Remark</strong> (this book contains anger)
> It is not anger at any particular person. It is anger at the fact that a problem that should have been visible was not sufficiently solved when I was a student, and has still not been sufficiently solved even now. Vector analysis implicitly contains the metric, orientation, duality, and the exterior derivative, yet it is taught as a collection of formulas while hiding them. Meanwhile, differential forms have been placed up in the heights of manifold theory, and have not been brought down as tools for beginners trying to understand vector analysis. This book is an attack on that gap, and at the same time, a proposed alternative.
