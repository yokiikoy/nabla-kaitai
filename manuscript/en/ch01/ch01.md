# Chapter 1: Deconstructing the Symbols of Differential and Integral Calculus with "Matrices"

#### §1.1 The Mathematician's One Dimension, the Physicist's One Dimension
When we learned the one-dimensional integral $\int f(x) dx$ in high school, the textbook described it with "$x$ a single line called the x-axis." That is perfectly fine for a mathematics textbook. <strong>Mathematicians</strong> assume a self-contained abstract world called one-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^1$ and build their logic upon it. A point is a real number $x$, a displacement is a real number $\Delta x$, and an integral is defined as the limit of "function times infinitesimal width." Everything is self-contained.

<strong>But we are physicists.</strong>---PARA--The <strong>real physical space</strong> we deal with is always three-dimensional. Even when a mass point appears to move along a straight line, it is not that it exists in a "fictional one-dimensional space"; rather, we are observing a "cross-section within three-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$ where displacements in the $y$ and $z$ directions happen to be unobserved (or negligible)."
For example, consider a cart sliding on a frictionless straight rail. We say "this is one-dimensional motion along the $x$-axis," but in reality:
* The rail is placed in three-dimensional space.
* The cart might be minutely vibrating up and down.
* There are also tiny lateral fluctuations due to air resistance.

What a physicist calls "one-dimensional motion" refers to a situation <strong>where, within three-dimensional space, displacement in a particular direction is dominant, and displacements in other directions are either negligibly small or constrained to be strictly zero by the symmetry of the system.</strong>
Therefore, the true nature of what a physicist calls a "one-dimensional infinitesimal displacement" is not merely a scalar $\Delta x$, but is more appropriately represented by a <strong>vertical vector (column vector)</strong> as follows:
$$\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

> <strong>Note</strong> (Non‑standard terminology) Throughout this book, I will use the terms <strong>vertical vector</strong> and __PH_0058__horizontal vector__PH_0058__ (please read vertical as column, horizontal as row). I have trouble distinguishing the words "row" and "column".

We call such a displacement from a point in three-dimensional space, expressed in three components, a <strong>displacement vector</strong>. Following convention, we take the <strong>first component to correspond to displacement in the $x$-axis direction, the second to the $y$-axis direction, and the third to the $z$-axis direction.</strong>

These zeros in the second and third components do not have the negative connotation of "being ignored" or "not existing." Rather, they are the result of an active choice: <strong>"we are currently focusing only on motion in the $x$-axis direction and have intentionally excluded the other components from the measurement."</strong>
This "three-dimensional premise as a physicist" is the key to revealing the true nature of the symbol $dx$ that clings to the end of the integral sign. In the next section, we will deconstruct $dx$ from an "infinitesimal quantity" into a "matrix" from this perspective. <strong></strong>

<strong>This book is intended solely as a textbook on elementary differential and integral calculus and vector analysis, and is not intended to develop differential forms in arbitrary dimensions. Therefore, we will remain fixed on the three-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system $(x,y,z)$ throughout, and adopt the most direct expression from linear algebra—the matrix representation. </strong>Consequently, we define $dx,dy,dz$ used in this book as a concrete matrix that extracts components in this coordinate system. The aim is first to immerse oneself in a straightforward vector analysis without deceit, using the simple tools of "three-dimensional, Cartesian, matrix."

> <strong>Note</strong> (Extension in Part III) However, in Part III, we will touch upon extensions to curvilinear coordinates and higher dimensions as needed.

### §1.2 A Vector Dissection of the Riemann Integral — Seeing Quadrature as a Matrix Operation
In the previous section, we reinterpreted physical infinitesimal displacements as vertical vectors $\mathbf{v}$. As in §1.1, we have
$$\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
With this view, let us now thoroughly deconstruct the process of the Riemann integral, which we have been familiar with since high school, and uncover the "matrix as $dx$" hidden within it.

---

> <strong>Note</strong> (Notation for transpose) In mathematics and other literature, the components of a vertical vector are sometimes written horizontally with a ${}^T$ superscript as an abbreviation. In this book, vertical vectors will always be written in the form $\begin{pmatrix}\cdots\end{pmatrix}$. When ${}^T$ appears, please read it as the symbol for the matrix transpose operation.

#### 1.2.1 Standard Construction of a Riemann Sum (Review)
The definite integral of a function $f(x)$ over the interval $[a, b]$ is defined as follows:

> <strong>Note</strong> (Closed interval) The notation $[a,b]$ denotes the <strong>closed interval</strong> (the set of real numbers $x$ such that $a \le x \le b$), which includes both endpoints $a$ and $b$. This is explicitly stated for readers not yet accustomed to the mathematician's convention.

1. Divide the interval into $n$ subintervals: $a = x_0 __PH_0077__中から__PH_0078__代表点 $.  
2. Choose one __PH_0482__ point $\xi_i$ in each subinterval.  
3. Form the Riemann sum $R_n = \sum_{i=1}^n f(\xi_i) \Delta x_i$ (the sum for this division is denoted $R_n$).  
4. Take the limit as the division becomes finer (strictly speaking, the <strong>maximum</strong> of the widths of the subintervals approaches $0$. Simply increasing the number of divisions $n$ can still leave some widths remaining, so mathematics emphasizes this condition. In the following, $\lim_{n\to\infty} R_n$ assumes divisions satisfying this condition): $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n\to\infty} R_n$

In this textbook explanation, $\Delta x_i$ is a one-dimensional quantity representing an "infinitesimal width." However, we should be able to go one step further.

#### 1.2.2 Displacement per Subinterval
The displacement in the $i$-th subinterval $[x_{i-1}, x_i]$ is, following the <strong>displacement vector</strong> of §1.1, written adapted to the width of that interval as
$$\mathbf{v}_i = \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x_i \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
The subscript $i$ means "for the $i$-th subinterval," and $\mathbf{v}_i$ is also a displacement vector. Since we are currently considering straight-line motion, the $y,z$ component is zero, but it is a result, not a condition; it simply happens to be zero.

#### 1.2.3 The Appearance of $dx$ — A "Assertion" as the Greatest Feature of This Book
Now, in this book, we assign the following meaning to the symbol $dx$. <strong>It may seem bold and strange, but this is also the greatest feature of this book—we assert that $dx$ is the following $1\times 3$ matrix.</strong>

That is, as a <strong>horizontal vector</strong> (written as a <strong> matrix</strong> with components arranged horizontally), we set
$$dx = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
This is the matrix representation of a linear map that takes an input vertical vector and returns only its $x$ component.

> <strong>Note</strong> (Convention for arranging components) For abbreviated notation of horizontal vectors (rows) or row components, we write them <strong>$(1\ 0\ 0)$</strong> <strong>without commas</strong>, simply separating the components by spaces. <strong>$(1, 0, 0)$</strong> When writing with <strong>a comma followed by a space</strong>, we use it to emphasize <strong>coordinates</strong> (positions of points, etc.), and <strong>do not use it for abbreviating matrices or horizontal vectors.</strong>

Multiplying from the left onto the displacement vector $\mathbf{v}_i$, we get
$$dx\,\mathbf{v}_i = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x_i \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \Delta x_i$$
Here, we adopt the convention of writing the idea "$dx$ eats the displacement vector $\mathbf{v}_i$ and spits out the scalar $\Delta x_i$" <strong>like a function value</strong>:
$$dx(\mathbf{v}_i) = dx\,\mathbf{v}_i = \Delta x_i$$
<strong>In this book, we will often emphasize this form $dx(\mathbf{v})$, writing "the horizontal vector $dx$ acts on the vertical vector $\mathbf{v}$." No matter how many times we repeat it, it cannot be overstated.</strong>

> <strong>Note</strong> (Naming of operator, operation, function, map) Whether we call this $dx(\mathbf{v})$ an <strong>operator</strong>, an <strong>operator</strong>, a <strong>function</strong>, or a <strong>mapping</strong> varies among different literatures. I prefer to call it an <strong>operator</strong>, so I will likely call it an operator from now on.

> <strong>Note</strong> (Coordinate-independent $dx$ and Cartesian representation) Mathematicians often speak of $dx$ as a "geometric entity independent of coordinates." The $\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\end{pmatrix}$ I just wrote is merely its representation in real space. <strong>I will properly discuss the fact that components change with location in §1.5</strong>, so please read on calmly.

The $\Delta x_i$ that appears in the Riemann sum is not a "mysterious one-dimensional infinitesimal quantity." It is <strong>the result of the above matrix $dx$ extracting only the $x$ component from the displacement vector $\mathbf{v}_i$ in three-dimensional space.</strong>

<strong>This is a decisive moment.</strong>
* Left-hand side: $dx(\mathbf{v}_i)$ is an algebraic operation where the matrix $dx$ acts on the <strong>displacement vector $\mathbf{v}_i$</strong>
* Right-hand side: The "width of subinterval $\Delta x_i$" appearing in the conventional Riemann sum.

In other words, $\Delta x_i$ is <strong>not a mysterious one-dimensional infinitesimal displacement, but rather the $dx(\mathbf{v}_i)$ obtained by extracting only the component in the $x$ direction from the three-dimensional infinitesimal displacement $\mathbf{v}_i$</strong>.

#### 1.2.4 Vector Reinterpretation of the Riemann Sum and the Integral Sign
Rewriting the Riemann sum from this perspective:
$$R_n = \sum_{i=1}^n f(\xi_i) \cdot dx(\mathbf{v}_i)$$

Furthermore, if we set for each subinterval
$$f(\xi_i)\,dx = \begin{pmatrix} f(\xi_i) & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
(the scalar $f(\xi_i)$ scales each row), then
$$\bigl(f(\xi_i)\,dx\bigr)(\mathbf{v}_i) = f(\xi_i) \cdot dx(\mathbf{v}_i) = f(\xi_i)\,\Delta x_i$$
and we obtain each term of the Riemann sum:
$$R_n = \sum_{i=1}^n \bigl(f(\xi_i)\,dx\bigr)(\mathbf{v}_i)$$

Taking the limit as the division becomes infinitely fine (in the sense that the maximum width approaches $0$, as in step 5), by the definition of the Riemann integral,
$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n\to\infty} R_n = \lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{i=1}^n \bigl(f(\xi_i)\,dx\bigr)(\mathbf{v}_i)$$
The $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ on the left-hand side is precisely the integral sign we have been familiar with since high school—that is, it is the same quantity as $\lim_{n\to\infty} R_n$ written in step 5 above. The right-hand side is another face of the same integral, viewed as <strong>the limit of summing, over each subinterval, the value obtained when the matrix $f(\xi_i)\,dx$ acts on the displacement vector.</strong>

#### 1.2.5 Physical Interpretation — Example of Work Calculation

> <strong>Note</strong> (For readers not familiar with mechanics) I do not intend to give a lecture on mechanics here. The $W=\int F\,dx$ given below is simply borrowed as a familiar example to <strong>show how to read an integral as the limit of a matrix action.</strong> You do not need to grasp the strict definition of force or the details of units to follow the main point of this section. Many of the intended readers will have studied mechanics at the high school level, but even novice readers will have no trouble if they understand the purpose above.

Consider the work done when a force $F(x)$ acts on a mass point moving along a straight line. Readers who have studied mechanics will be familiar with the expression
$$W = \int_a^b F(x)\,dx$$
In the style of this book, using matrix representation,
$$W = \lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{i=1}^n \begin{pmatrix} F(\xi_i) & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x_i \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{i=1}^n \bigl(F(\xi_i)\,dx\bigr)(\mathbf{v}_i) = \int_a^b F(x)\,dx$$
From left to right: <strong>the limit of the Riemann sum with components explicitly written</strong>, <strong>the same limit in operator notation</strong>, and <strong>the conventional integral sign.</strong> They represent <strong>the same quantity</strong> in three different ways. Here, $\mathbf{v}_i = \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x_i \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is a scalar (the x-component of the force).

$F(x)\,dx = \begin{pmatrix} F(x) & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is an operator, and $\bigl(F(x)\,dx\bigr)(\mathbf{v}) = F(x)\,\Delta x$ becomes the infinitesimal work as a first-order approximation.

#### 1.2.6 Linear Forms ($1$-forms)

So far, we have defined $dx$ as a "horizontal vector that acts on a displacement vector, extracts a specific component, and returns a scalar (real number)." Such a <strong>linear</strong> measuring device that takes a vector as input and outputs a scalar is technically called a <strong>1-form ($1$-form)</strong>. What we have been calling the "matrix $dx$" in this paper is precisely this 1-form.

> <strong>Note</strong> (Term "covector") Mathematicians often use the term <strong>covector</strong> as another name for a <strong>1-form ($1$-form)</strong>. You can keep this in mind as a reference when reading other books.

The origin of the name is simple:

* <strong>"Linear"</strong>: because it processes the displacement vector in a <strong>linear</strong> way.
* <strong>"Form"</strong>: meaning "form of measurement" or "form of action."

From now on, we will call this measuring device either a "matrix" or a "1-form ($1$-form)." Both terms refer to the same entity.

<strong>Notation Convention of This Book</strong>

<strong>We will not use $dx$ alone to mean "infinitesimal displacement" or "infinitesimal change."</strong> To avoid confusion between the width of displacement and the measuring device (horizontal vector), we agree as follows. For the magnitude of a (infinitesimal) displacement in the $x$ direction, we use <strong>$\Delta x$</strong>, or explicitly write the displacement vector $\mathbf{v}$ and write <strong>$dx(\mathbf{v})$</strong>.

On the other hand, <strong>a $dx$ appearing alone</strong> refers to the operator as a horizontal vector (1-form) that <strong>extracts the $x$ component.</strong> The $dx$ that appears in expressions like $df = f'(x)\,dx$ are <strong>always read as operators.</strong> <strong>Thoroughly maintaining this distinction is the foundation for all subsequent understanding.</strong>

We retain the $dx$ at the end of the integral sign $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ as a <strong>conventional notation</strong> (a "block" paired with the integration variable $x$). In this book, we will often not expand this into $dx(\mathbf{v})$ every time, but <strong>in the main text discussing displacement and infinitesimal widths, we will align with $\Delta x$ or $dx(\mathbf{v})$, and do not attach the image of width or change to a bare $dx$</strong>.

> <strong>Note</strong> (Usage of $dx$ in literature) Physicists often use the convention of writing the component of displacement itself as $dx$. However, once you get used to the notation of this book, <strong>it becomes easier to read with a clear distinction between $dx$ as a measuring device and the scalar of displacement.</strong> In more advanced books, this distinction will come into play directly.

In the next section, we will extend this perspective to the differential of a function and define the total differential $df$ as a matrix.

> <strong>【Checkpoint so far】</strong>
> - Infinitesimal displacement is a vertical vector $\mathbf{v}$. $\Delta x$ is a scalar width, and a standalone $dx$ is an operator as a horizontal vector (1-form).
> - The Cartesian $dy$, $dz$ will be set in <strong>next section §1.3.3</strong> (they are <strong>1-forms of the same idea as</strong> $dx$ in §1.2.6). The distinction from $\Delta y$, $\Delta z$ and $dy(\mathbf{v})$, $dz(\mathbf{v})$ follows the <strong>notation convention</strong> in §1.2.6.
> - Each term of the Riemann sum has the form $(f\,dx)(\mathbf{v}_i)$, and the integral can be understood as its limit.
> - The $dx$ at the end of the integral sign $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ is a conventional notation; the displacement itself is written as $\Delta x$ or $dx(\mathbf{v})$ (convention in §1.2.6).

### §1.3 Total Differential $df$ — An Operator That Packs Rates of Change into a Matrix
In the previous section, we dissected the Riemann integral as the "limit of a sum of actions of the matrix $f(x)\,dx$ on displacement vectors." In this section, we extend this idea to the derivative of a function itself, and <strong>define the total differential $df$ as a horizontal vector (matrix).</strong> The "derivative" is treated not as a mere numerical rate of change, but as an operator that, <strong>when multiplied by a displacement vector, yields the linear part of the change.</strong>
#### 1.3.1 Matrix Representation of Differentiability
A function $f(x)$ is said to be differentiable at a point $x$ if the following first-order approximation holds:
$$\Delta f = f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) = f'(x) \Delta x + o(|\Delta x|) \quad (|\Delta x| \to 0)$$

---

> <strong>Note</strong> (Landau order notation) <strong>$o(|\Delta x|)$</strong> is <strong>Landau order notation.</strong> When $\Delta x \to 0$, the quantity written collectively as $o(|\Delta x|)$ means a remainder that approaches zero <strong>much faster than $|\Delta x|$</strong>, and it is <strong>negligibly small</strong> compared to the principal term $f'(x)\Delta x$. This is common in science and engineering textbooks, but some readers may be seeing it for the first time.

Here, $\Delta x$ is a scalar, but we reinterpret it as a three-dimensional displacement vector as in the previous section:
$$\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

Then the change in the function, $\Delta f$, can be seen as the effect induced by this $\mathbf{v}$.

#### 1.3.2 Definition of $df$ as a Matrix and $df(\mathbf{v})$
The <strong>total differential</strong> $df$ of a function $f$ at a point $x$ is defined as the following $1 \times 3$ horizontal vector:
$$df = f'(x) \, dx = \begin{pmatrix} f'(x) & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

The result of applying this $df$ to a displacement $\mathbf{v}$ is written as <strong>$df(\mathbf{v})$</strong>, similar to the previous section:
$$df(\mathbf{v}) = \begin{pmatrix} f'(x) & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = f'(x)\,\Delta x$$

<strong>An important shift in perspective</strong> (<strong>always read as follows</strong>):
* The expression $df = f'(x)\,dx$ is the familiar <strong>notation for the total differential</strong> in calculus, but in this book, <strong>$df$ and $dx$ are both horizontal vectors as operators,</strong> and <strong>by themselves they are not infinitesimal changes.</strong>
* When we write <strong>$df(\mathbf{v})$</strong>, it means <strong>the change as a first-order approximation for a finite (but sufficiently small) displacement $\mathbf{v}$</strong>.

$df$ itself is not a change. It is a <strong>"device" that generates the linear part of a change.</strong>

> <strong>Note</strong> ($df$ is an operator, $df(\mathbf{v})$ is the change) I repeat the same distinction as in the notation convention of §1.2.6. You may find it tedious, but <strong>misreading this will cause everything to go wrong from here on,</strong> so repetition is worthwhile.

#### 1.3.3 Rulers in the $y$ and $z$ Directions: $dy$, $dz$

The strength of this perspective, when we return to the mechanical example in §1.2.5, is that it <strong>naturally extends to the general case where the force also has components in the $y$ directions.</strong>
<strong>Using the now-defined $dy$</strong>, we can treat two-dimensional work uniformly in the form $F_x dx + F_y dy$.
Just as $dx$ extracts the $x$ component, in real space we define **$dy$ as extracting the $y$ component** and **$dz$ as extracting the $z$ component** as horizontal vectors (1-forms). The matrix representations are
$$dy = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad dz = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$
For a displacement $\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x \\ \Delta y \\ \Delta z \end{pmatrix}$, we have $dy(\mathbf{v}) = \Delta y$, $dz(\mathbf{v}) = \Delta z$.

> <strong>Note</strong> (Convention for $dy$, $dz$) Standalone $dy$, $dz$ are operators; when speaking of infinitesimal widths of $y$ or $z$, we write $\Delta y$, $\Delta z$ or $dy(\mathbf{v})$, $dz(\mathbf{v})$. The $dy$, $dz$ that appear at the end of the integral sign should be read in the <strong>same way as $dx$,</strong> following the conventional notation from high school. The detailed conventions apply <strong>the same idea</strong> as the <strong>notation convention</strong> summarized for $dx$ in §1.2.6.

Three-dimensional space is equipped with **three rulers**. In this chapter, for the main line of explanation, we have focused on the cross-section in the $x$ direction, but please consider that the coordinates $y,z$ and the probes $dy,dz$ are there from the beginning.
Now, we have added $dy$, $dz$ to the single-variable $df=f'(x)\,dx$ as rulers of the same type. In the following concrete example and in §1.3.6's extension to three dimensions, we will see how these three work.

#### 1.3.4 Concrete Example: The Case of $f(x) = x^2$
Let $f(x) = x^2$. Then $f'(x) = 2x$ is a 1-form (horizontal vector):
$$df = 6\,dx = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
Therefore, the product of the matrices is
$$df(\mathbf{v}) = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0.1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = 6\cdot 0.1 + 0\cdot 0 + 0\cdot 0 = 0.6$$

Indeed, $f(3.1) - f(3) = 9.61 - 9 = 0.61$, and the first-order approximation $0.6$ matches well.

#### 1.3.5 Matrix Interpretation of Integration by Substitution

> <strong>Note</strong> (How to read on) This section is an application of the perspective "a 1-form acts on a displacement" developed in §1.2. It is not essential for progressing to the main line in §1.3.6 and beyond (the multi-dimensional extension of $df$). If you are not interested, you may proceed to §1.3.6.

Integration by substitution is an operation that changes the integration variable and rewrites the integrand (as covered in high school mathematics). The purpose of this section is <strong>not to memorize a single formula.</strong> In the language developed up to §1.2, it is about showing the structure that <strong>a Riemann sum where the 1-form $F\,dx$ on the space side acts on each step's displacement $\Delta\mathbf{r}$</strong> and <strong>the step width $\Delta t$ on the time parameter $t$</strong> are <strong>connected within the same step,</strong> naturally leading to the form of the substituted integral $\int F(x(t))\,\frac{dx}{dt}\,dt$.

Take work as an example. Work can be written as
$$W = \int_a^b F(x)\,dx$$
(As we saw in §1.2.5, the same quantity can be viewed as the limit of a matrix action). Suppose the position moves with time $t$, and we denote the trajectory from <t_1$ で $x(t_0)=a,\; x(t_1)=b$ となるようにパラメータ表示すると、空間内の点は

と書ける。

<strong>one step on the time axis</strong> by a vertical one‑component vector
$$\mathbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix} \Delta t \end{pmatrix}$$
If we <strong>consider them side by side,</strong> we place a "measuring device" that extracts only the $t$ component as a $1\times 1$ matrix
$$dt = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \end{pmatrix}$$
and read it as $dt(\mathbf{w})=\Delta t$. This is a <strong>pattern</strong> of "horizontal = measuring device, vertical = step, action gives scalar" from §1.2.6, but compressed for time alone.

> <strong>Note</strong> (Time-axis $dt$ — difference from spatial $1$-form) Pardon me — $dt$ is a $1\times 1$ horizontal vector, and it is a measuring device on a different space from the $3$-form defined on the spatial $\mathbb{R}^3$ of dimension $dx$. Geometrically, $dx$ is a $1$‑form belonging to the cotangent space of three‑dimensional space, while $dt$ is a $1$‑form belonging to the cotangent space of the one‑dimensional parameter axis of time. <strong>Strictly speaking, the introduction so far in this book does not fully cover the relationship between the two.</strong> <strong>However, as a physicist, time cannot be completely embedded in the three dimensions of space — I could not cheat by rounding it into a purely spatial story.</strong> Therefore, I cut out the axis of $t$ as $1\times1$ and place it squarely. Since it is aligned with the <strong>same pattern</strong> as $dx$, i.e., horizontal = measuring device, vertical = infinitesimal step, action gives scalar, it should be easy to understand.

<strong>Organizing the correspondence between the space side and the time side</strong>, we have the following table. The same pattern is simply repeated in different dimensions.

|  | Space side | Time side |
|---|---|---|
| <strong>Horizontal vector (measuring device)</strong> | $F(x)\,dx$ ($1\times 3$) | $dt$ ($1\times 1$) |
| <strong>Vertical vector (displacement)</strong> | $\mathbf{v}_i = \Delta\mathbf{r}$ | $\mathbf{w} = \Delta t$ |
| <strong>Result of action</strong> | $(F\,dx)(\mathbf{v}_i) = F\,\Delta x$ | $dt(\mathbf{w}) = \Delta t$ |

The corresponding <strong>actual spatial displacement</strong> for this $\Delta t$ is
$$\Delta \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}(t+\Delta t) - \mathbf{r}(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Delta x = x(t+\Delta t)-x(t)$$
Here, let $\Delta t \neq 0$. The quotient $\Delta x/\Delta t$ is <strong>the average rate of change of $x$ in that step.</strong> The identity known even to middle school students
$$\Delta x = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\,\Delta t$$
holds. Therefore, we can write
$$\begin{aligned}
\Delta \mathbf{r} &= \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\,\Delta t \\
&= \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\,dt(\mathbf{w})
\end{aligned}$$
<strong>This is not an approximation nor a magical calculus trick; it is simply a rewriting that inserts the quotient of $\Delta x$ divided by $\Delta t$ in between.</strong> The left vertical vector is "the average rate of change in the $x$ direction for that step," and the right $dt(\mathbf{w})=\Delta t$ is the width of the time step.

Now, since $F\,dx$ is a horizontal vector (operator), at each step we obtain a <strong>scalar</strong>
$$\bigl(F(x)\,dx\bigr)(\Delta \mathbf{r}) = F(x)\,\Delta x = F(x)\,\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\,\Delta t = F(x)\,\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\,dt(\mathbf{w})$$
The $dt$ at the end of the integral sign $\int_{t_0}^{t_1} g(t)\,dt$ is, like the $dx$ in $\int f(x)\,dx$ of §1.2.6, a <strong>conventional notation paired with the integration variable.</strong> At the stage of the Riemann sum, it should be read as <strong>$\Delta t$ corresponds to $dt(\mathbf{w})$</strong>.

As we refine the division and take the limit $\Delta t \to 0$, the average rate of change $\Delta x/\Delta t$ in each step approaches the slope of the tangent line $\dfrac{dx}{dt}$. Therefore, we arrive at the high school integration‑by‑substitution form
$$W = \int_a^b F(x)\,dx = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} F(x(t))\,\frac{dx}{dt}\,dt$$
(where $\dfrac{dx}{dt}$ is the limit of $\Delta x/\Delta t$). Up to just before, we had a <strong>identity</strong> rewriting using the average rate of change $\Delta x/\Delta t$, but <strong>as a first‑order approximation when each step's $\Delta t$ is sufficiently small,</strong> evaluating the average rate of change by the tangent slope $\dfrac{dx}{dt}$, the typical Riemann term inside the limit reads
$$\bigl(F(x(t))\,dx\bigr)(\Delta \mathbf{r}) \approx F(x(t))\,\frac{dx}{dt}\,\Delta t$$
which corresponds to a structure where <strong>the value obtained when the 1-form $F\,dx$ on the space side acts on $\Delta\mathbf{r}$</strong> and <strong>the steps on the time side $\Delta t$ (i.e., $dt(\mathbf{w})$) are accumulated.</strong>

#### 1.3.6 Extension to Three Dimensions

A function $f$ that assigns a scalar (real number) to each point $(x,y,z)$ in space can be treated as a <strong>real‑valued function</strong> on an open set of real space.

> <strong>Note</strong> (Scalar field) In physics, such a function is often called a <strong>scalar field.</strong>

That $f(x,y,z)$ is <strong> (totally) differentiable</strong> at a point $(x,y,z)$ means that for a displacement $\mathbf{v}=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta x\\\Delta y\\\Delta z\end{pmatrix}$, we have
$$\Delta f = f(x+\Delta x,\,y+\Delta y,\,z+\Delta z) - f(x,y,z) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\,\Delta z + o(\|\mathbf{v}\|) \quad (\|\mathbf{v}\|\to 0)$$
This aligns with the definition for a single variable in §1.3.1. $o(\|\mathbf{v}\|)$ is an abbreviation for the remainder term (same intent as $o(|\Delta x|)$); we will not go into depth here, but it should not hinder understanding.

Using the rulers $dx,dy,dz$ from §1.3.3, we define an operator of the same type as in §1.3.2:
$$df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dx + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,dy + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\,dz = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \end{pmatrix}$$
In the very first formula, the $o(\|\mathbf{v}\|)$ is omitted here for a few lines, and we <strong>follow only the skeleton of the notation.</strong> When $\mathbf{v}=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta x\\\Delta y\\\Delta z\end{pmatrix}$, from the definition of differentiability we have $\Delta f = df(\mathbf{v}) + o(\|\mathbf{v}\|)$ — that is, <strong>$df(\mathbf{v})$ is not the exact change $\Delta f$ itself, but its linear (principal) part excluding the remainder.</strong> Below, we write out this principal part as a matrix product:
$$df(\mathbf{v}) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta x\\\Delta y\\\Delta z\end{pmatrix} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta x\\\Delta y\\\Delta z\end{pmatrix} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta x\\\Delta y\\\Delta z\end{pmatrix}$$
$$= \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\,\Delta z.$$
The first line shows, by a <strong>matrix product,</strong> how the ruler $dx,dy,dz$ from §1.3.3, as a row vector, extracts the $x,y,z$ component from $\mathbf{v}$, and the partial derivative function multiplies as a coefficient. The second line is its evaluation result, which has the same form as the <strong>linear principal part</strong> in the definition of $\Delta f$ earlier. The reading <strong>horizontal vector (operator) × vertical vector (displacement) → scalar</strong> from §1.3.2 remains unchanged in three dimensions.

Even a function $f(x,y)$ where only two coordinates appear can be included in the above framework as $\partial f/\partial z=0$ if we do not depend on $z$ — <strong>the framework placed in three dimensions degenerates as it is into lower dimensions.</strong>

Let us compare:
For one variable: $df = f'(x) dx = \begin{pmatrix} f'(x) & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$
For a two‑variable function $f(x, y)$ (using $dy$ from §1.3.3): $df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} dx + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} dy = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} & 0 \end{pmatrix}$
For three variables $f(x,y,z)$, as in the formula in §1.3.6, the horizontal vector simply has three components.

Thus, <strong>the form is the same, only the number of components increases.</strong> This is the power of matrix representation. By regarding $df$ as a matrix, the differential can be treated uniformly as an operator that gives a linear approximation to the displacement.

#### 1.3.7 Preview of Line Integral
When a curve in space is expressed as $\mathbf{r}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}x(t)\\y(t)\\z(t)\end{pmatrix}$ with a parameter $t$, and the displacement in a small step is written as $\Delta\mathbf{r}$, the operation of summing $\bigl(df\bigr)(\Delta\mathbf{r})$ over each step and taking the limit is symbolically expressed as
$$\int_\gamma df$$
(where $\gamma$ is the curve). As with $\int f'(x)\,dx$ we saw in §1.3.5, the skeleton is <strong>a Riemann sum where the 1-form $df$ acts on the displacement of each step.</strong> A rigorous formulation involving closed curves, orientation, and reparametrization will be <strong>deferred to later chapters.</strong>

> <strong>Note</strong> (Line integrals and Stokes in later chapters) The framework of line integrals, restriction of $1$-forms to curves, and Stokes' theorem will be set up mainly in Chapter 3 and beyond. In Chapter 2, we first construct measuring devices for area and volume ($2$-form, $3$-form). This is only a preview of motivation and notation.

> <strong>【Checkpoint so far】</strong>
> - Differentiability is captured as a first-order approximation expressed by $\Delta f = f'(x)\Delta x + o(|\Delta x|)$ ($o$ is Landau order notation).
> - $df = f'(x)\,dx$ is a horizontal vector; the change in the numerical value is read as $df(\mathbf{v})$.
> - Integration by substitution can also be seen as a correspondence between the structure of the 1-form $F\,dx$ acting on spatial displacement and the accumulation of $dt$ on the time axis.

### §1.4 Leibniz Notation and Algebraic Intuition

---

When Leibniz introduced the symbols $dx$, $dy$, he treated them intuitively as "infinitesimals."

---

> <strong>Note</strong> (Leibniz and the symbols) <strong>Leibniz</strong> (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, 1646–1716) was the mathematician who established the symbolic system of calculus including $dx$, $dy$. Below, we will not delve into historical details but will only mention his name as the person who introduced the symbols (many readers are familiar with the symbol $dx$, but if you do not recognize the name, this one line suffices).

We moderns can say that we have <strong>rephrased his intuition in the language of linear algebra.</strong>
Leibniz's notation $df = f'(x)dx$ is not just a formal equality:
* $dx$: Leibniz's intuition of infinitesimals → In this book: an <strong>operator</strong> as component extraction (when speaking of the infinitesimal displacement itself, we use $\Delta x$ or $dx(\mathbf{v})$). $dy$, $dz$ are similarly defined in §1.3.3.
* $df$: Leibniz's intuition of infinitesimal change → In this book: the total differential as an <strong>operator</strong> (the change in the numerical value is $df(\mathbf{v})$ etc.)

<strong>Leibniz's genius was to see that differential and integral calculus are essentially algebraic operations.</strong> We have merely given his intuition a concrete skeleton in terms of matrices.

In the next section, we will clarify the implicit assumption on which this framework depends — the "convenience" of the Cartesian coordinate system — and suggest a connection to more general coordinates.

> <strong>【Checkpoint so far】</strong>
> - Leibniz's intuitions of $dx$ and $df$ are both repositioned in this book as "horizontal vectors (operators) that act on displacement."
> - When speaking of infinitesimal changes, we use $\Delta x$ or $df(\mathbf{v})$, and we do not confuse the roles of the symbols.

### §1.5 Coordinate Transformations — Rewriting the Scale of the "Rulers"

---

Throughout this chapter, we have discussed the <strong>matrix representation</strong> of $dx$ as $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. The reason this is constant regardless of location is that we are measuring physical space with the most straightforward scale: <strong>Cartesian coordinates (orthogonal rectilinear coordinates).</strong>

---

However, the reality of physics is not always rectangular. For problems such as flow in a circular pipe or the magnetic field around a straight current, it is far more convenient to use <strong>cylindrical coordinates</strong>, which specify a point by a triple of numbers $(r,\theta,z)$. So, what does the "ruler that extracts components," $dx$, look like in a different coordinate system?

#### 1.5.1 The Chain Rule Gives the Component Representation of the "Ruler"

Recall the transformation equations between coordinate systems.

We incorporate this transformation rule into the framework of the <strong>total differential ($d$ as an operator)</strong> defined in §1.3.2. The <strong>total differential $dx$</strong> of the coordinate function $x(r,\theta,z)=r\cos\theta$ (which, again, is an operator as a 1-form, not the "infinitesimal change" of §1.2.6) expands as follows via the chain rule:

Here, all $dx, dr, d\theta, dz$ are <strong>operators (1-forms).</strong> What the right-hand side tells us is the fact that <strong>"when we re-express the real-space ruler $dx$ using $(dr,d\theta,dz)$ as a scale, it becomes a matrix with coefficients $(\cos\theta,\,-r\sin\theta,\,0)$ that vary from place to place."</strong>

$$x = r\cos\theta,\qquad y = r\sin\theta,\qquad z = z$$

In real space, $dx$ had constant components $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ under $(x,y,z)$. But as soon as we change the scale, it transforms into a matrix whose components are functions that depend on the location $(r,\theta)$. Although the object itself is invariant, "if you write it in a different coordinate system, its component representation also changes" — this is the algebraic essence of coordinate transformations.

$$dx = \frac{\partial x}{\partial r}dr + \frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta}d\theta + \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}dz = \cos\theta\,dr - r\sin\theta\,d\theta$$

> <strong>Note</strong> (Chain rule and change of variables — a hint for Chapter 3) Above, we used the chain rule for multivariable functions $dx = \frac{\partial x}{\partial r}dr + \frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta}d\theta + \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}dz$ as a computational rule. In this book, we have <strong>not yet formally formulated the change of variables for differential forms.</strong> A rigorous meaning of this formula, i.e., the general theory of "writing in a different coordinate system," will be treated again in §3.6 with the concept of <strong>pullback.</strong> For now, just get a sense that "the components of the matrix come out mechanically" and that the reader's existing computational experience and the style of "horizontal vector $dx$ acts on displacement" from this chapter will later connect.

$$dx \;\xrightarrow{\text{円柱座標の目盛りで書くと}} \;\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

> <strong>Note</strong> (Convention when reading as a matrix product) When multiplying the horizontal vector $\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ from the left, the partner is not the Cartesian components $\begin{pmatrix}\Delta x\\\Delta y\\\Delta z\end{pmatrix}$, but the vertical vector $\begin{pmatrix}\Delta r\\\Delta\theta\\\Delta z\end{pmatrix}$ that arranges the changes of the cylindrical coordinate parameters. Since we have rewritten the measuring device (1-form) in terms of the cylindrical coordinate scale, it is a matter of course that the displacement being measured must also be input in the same scale. In the exercise in §1.6, you will manually confirm that this product coincides with $dx(\mathbf{v})$.

#### 1.5.2 Letting Each Step of the Matrix Carry Geometry

Many textbooks draw a "curved grid" of $(r,\theta)$ in real space and explain geometrically that "the arc length in the $\theta$ direction is about $r\Delta\theta$." This intuition itself is correct—the problem lies in using such pictures as the foundation for "proof." As coordinate systems become more complicated, geometric "estimates" based on curved figures quickly hit their limits.

What this book aims to do is not to replace geometry with algebra. It is to <strong>break down the overall geometry into small pieces of geometry, each contained in each component of the matrix.</strong> The coefficient $-r\sin\theta$ carries, as a single number, self-contained geometric information: "how much a step in the $x$ direction contributes to the $\theta$ direction in real space." Similarly, $\cos\theta$ shows the effect from the $r$ direction to the $x$ direction, and $0$ indicates that the $z$ direction does not affect $x$. In other words, the single row $dx = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ decomposes the geometry of displacements in the $x$ components into <strong>three independent small problems.</strong> Geometric intuition and algebraic clarity are compatible in matrices.

#### 1.5.3 Two Cartesian Coordinate Systems

Here, let us clarify the relationship between the two triples of numbers we are currently dealing with — the real-space $(x,y,z)$ and the cylindrical coordinates $(r,\theta,z)$. On the calculation sheet, <strong>both are Cartesian coordinate systems with mutually orthogonal, equally spaced axes.</strong> The $\theta$ axis physically represents an "angle," but mathematically it is just a single straight number line.

Both real space and the parameter space of cylindrical coordinates are "flat graph paper." The only difference between them is whether partial derivative coefficients (conversion factors) such as $\cos\theta$ and $-r\sin\theta$ are inserted between them. During calculations, it is clearer to think not as "there is a curved coordinate axis in real space" but as "there is a translation rule between the two spaces."

> <strong>Note</strong> (Matrix representations of $dr$, $d\theta$) You might also wonder, "what is the matrix representation of $dr$ or $d\theta$ as numerical vectors?" Since the parameter space is also a Cartesian coordinate system, the answer is simple: $dr$ is the $(1\ 0\ 0)$ that extracts the first component in the parameter space, $d\theta$ is $(0\ 1\ 0)$, and $dz$ is $(0\ 0\ 1)$. They look the same as $dx=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\end{pmatrix}$ written in Cartesian real space, but they belong to a different coordinate system. A mathematician would chant pedantic spells like "$\omega \in \Omega^1(M)$, locally $\omega = \omega_i(x)\,dx^i$, coordinate system changes, $\phi^*\omega$, compatibility on overlapping open sets..." <strong>Indeed, my head starts to ache. Both you and I would get tired. Let us not go too deep.</strong>

#### 1.5.4 A Hint for Chapter 3 — Pullback

The operation of converting the measuring device of the original world for use with a different scale is technically called a <strong>pullback.</strong> For now, it is enough to grasp the sense that "the components of the matrix can be mechanically rewritten using partial derivatives." In Chapter 3, when we pull back the rulers for area and volume all at once, you will become convinced of the overwhelming power of this algebraic approach.

> <strong>Note</strong> (The word "metric" not used yet) In this book, we postpone the formal introduction of the <strong>metric</strong> until Chapter 5. The conversion factors $\cos\theta$ and $-r\sin\theta$ that appear here will be systematically collected as the metric $g$ in Chapter 5, but for now it is sufficient to treat them simply as "partial derivative coefficients coming from the chain rule." Rather than relying on the geometric intuition of drawing curved figures in real space and estimating edge lengths as "arc length $r\Delta\theta$," we will derive everything from the grid in parameter space and the transformation formulas alone — this algebra-first stance is the consistent attitude of this book.

> <strong>【Checkpoint so far】</strong>
> - The measuring device $dx$ itself is invariant, but when we change the coordinate system, its matrix representation (components) changes.
> - The coefficients of a coordinate transformation are found mechanically from the chain rule (partial derivatives), not from geometric pictures.
> - Since all effects of coordinate changes appear in the components of the matrix, we can always proceed with the computation using "straight" variables.
> - <strong>Check:</strong> In real-space $(x,y,z)$, $dx = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. How is it written with the cylindrical coordinate scale $(dr,d\theta,dz)$? Look back at §1.5.1.

### §1.6 Writing in Another Coordinate System — Exercise

---

At the end of this chapter, I want you to experience "writing in another coordinate system" once, as a calculation with your own hands. Although we have <strong>not yet defined a general theory of coordinate changes,</strong> as we saw in §1.5.3, <strong>the matrix representation of the same $dx$ changes depending on how the coordinates are taken.</strong> This exercise is to confirm that feeling by hand. <strong>Why do I want you to do this calculation before having the full rigorous language of coordinate changes?</strong> — so that later, when the formulas become busy all at once, you will have the settled conviction that "it's natural for components to change."

---

#### 1.6.1 Exercise: Measurement in Cylindrical Coordinates

<strong>Problem Setting</strong>  
As we saw in the previous section, the matrix representation of $dx$ in cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$ is as follows:
$$
dx = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$
(The row above lists the coefficients from the expansion of $(dr,d\theta,dz)$ in §1.5.3. Compared to the Cartesian representation $\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\end{pmatrix}$ in §1.2.3, <strong>the meaning of the vertical vector that stands on the right is different.</strong>)

Answer the following questions.

<strong>Question 1</strong>  
Suppose a mass point is at a point $P(r=2, \theta=\pi/6, z=0)$ in the cylindrical coordinate space. This mass point undergoes a small displacement $0.1$ in the $r$ direction. <strong>In this section, let the notation $\mathbf{v}$ denote the vertical vector that arranges the changes of the parameters $r,\theta,z$</strong> (this is a different arrangement from the Cartesian column $\begin{pmatrix}\Delta x\\\Delta y\\\Delta z\end{pmatrix}$ in §1.1–§1.3). Express this displacement vector $\mathbf{v}$ in terms of its components.

<strong>Question 2</strong>  
Apply the matrix $dx$ at the point $P$ to the displacement vector $\mathbf{v}$ from Question 1. (Use $\cos(\pi/6) = \sqrt{3}/2$.)

<strong>Question 3</strong>  
Explain physically what the result $dx(\mathbf{v})$ from Question 2 means.

#### 1.6.2 Solutions and Commentary

<strong>Solution to Question 1</strong>  
The displacement is $0.1$ in the $r$ direction and zero in the $\theta$ and $z$ directions. Therefore, <strong>as a vertical vector listing the changes in the cylindrical coordinate parameters,</strong> it can be written as follows:
$$
\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

<strong>Solution to Question 2</strong>  
The matrix $dx$ at the point $P$ is obtained by substituting $\theta = \pi/6, r=2$ into $1\times 3$, giving the row vector
$$
dx = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\frac{\pi}{6}) & -2\sin(\frac{\pi}{6}) & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} & -2 \times \frac{1}{2} & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$
(In cylindrical coordinates, the components of $dx$ depend on $r,\theta$, so here we refer to the evaluation at the point $P$. The notation $dx(\mathbf{v})$ for the action on $\mathbf{v}$ is the same as since §1.2.3.)  
Importantly, the second component generally has the coefficient <strong>$-r\sin\theta$</strong>, which has the <strong>dimension of length</strong> due to $r$. When we substitute $r=2,\;\theta=\pi/6$, we see the coefficient value $-1$, but <strong>the dimension is not attached to the numerical value $-1$ itself.</strong> It is accurate to read that the $-r\sin\theta$ as a component of the matrix carries the dimension of length. This is key for dimensional analysis.
Acting this on the displacement vector $\mathbf{v}$ gives:
$$
dx(\mathbf{v}) = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0.1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = 0.1 \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.0866
$$

<strong>Solution to Question 3 (Physical meaning)</strong>  
This result expresses the physical fact that <strong>"a motion that goes outward (in the $r$ direction) by $0.1$ in cylindrical coordinates corresponds, when viewed from the real-space $x$-axis direction, to a forward movement of approximately $0.0866$."</strong>

> <strong>Note</strong> ("Approximately" vs. exact equality) For this displacement, $\Delta\theta=\Delta z=0$, so from $x=r\cos\theta$ we have $\Delta x=(\Delta r)\cos\theta$ <strong>exactly.</strong> Therefore, the value of $dx(\mathbf{v})$ coincides with the true $\Delta x$ without error <strong>under this displacement.</strong> The "approximately" is merely a rounding when representing $\sqrt{3}/2$ as a decimal; it is not an error of the first-order approximation.

#### 1.6.3 A Deep Look at Dimensional Analysis

Looking at the computation result $dx(\mathbf{v}) = 0.1 \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, it is the product of the $r$component$0.1$ (dimension of length) and the coefficient of the first component $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ (dimensionless), so the output indeed has the dimension of length.
However, if we input a displacement in the $\theta$ direction $\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0.1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ (angular displacement $0.1$ radians) (the coefficient is still the same row vector evaluated at the point $P$):
$$dx(\mathbf{v}) = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0.1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = -0.1$$
In this case, the <strong>second component of the input vector</strong> $0.1$ is an angle (dimensionless), but the <strong>coefficient corresponding to the second column of the matrix</strong> $-r\sin\theta$ carries the dimension of length, so the output $-0.1$ as a product has the dimension of length.

Here lies the <strong>remarkable property of a 1-form:</strong> The matrix components of $dx$ themselves are functions involving $r$, and even if the input consists of coordinate components (which may have different dimensions), the output is automatically adjusted to always have the correct physical dimension of "length in the $x$ direction." <strong>A 1-form is a "smart measuring device" that absorbs the distortion of the coordinate system and outputs a physically meaningful measurement.</strong>
